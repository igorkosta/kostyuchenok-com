# The Challengers Became the Incumbents. The Incumbents Became Irrelevant.

The "challenger bank" narrative is dead. Not because the challengers failed. Because they won.

Revolut made $2.3 billion in profit in 2025. Monzo is on its third consecutive year of profitability. Starling is on its fifth. These are not startups burning venture capital. They are profitable banks with banking licences, regulatory approval, and millions of customers choosing them over institutions that have existed for centuries.

Meanwhile, the banks that were supposed to be unassailable — the incumbents — are the ones struggling to keep customers. Santander lost nearly 20,000 net customers in a single quarter while spending £5.62 million a month on paid search. The incumbents have become the challenged. And the challenged are being left behind.

## The numbers tell the story.

The instinct of every incumbent board is to point at total assets and total customer counts. "We have 26 million customers. They have 15 million." That framing is already outdated.

| | Revolut | Monzo | Starling | Lloyds | NatWest | Santander UK |
|---|---------|-------|----------|--------|---------|-------------|
| Customers | 68.3M | 15M+ | 9M+ | 26M | 19M | 14M |
| Revenue (2025) | $6B | £1.7B | £1.7B | £18B | £14B | £6B |
| Profit (2025) | $2.3B | £172M | £1.3B | £5.5B | £4.3B | £2B |
| Growth rate | 46% YoY | 39% YoY | ~20% YoY | ~5% YoY | ~5% YoY | ~3% YoY |

The incumbents are bigger. But the challengers are faster, more efficient, and growing at ten times the rate. Revolut added 16 million new customers in 2025 alone — nearly as many as Santander's entire UK customer base. Monzo's gross profit surpassed £1 billion for the first time. Starling generated £1.3 billion in profit from a fraction of the headcount.

And the economics are about to get worse. Bloomberg Intelligence calculated that Revolut's UK banking licence — finally awarded in March 2026 — is projected to erode £400 million in annual net interest income from the UK's big four. Every £10 billion in current account outflows costs incumbents up to £375 million per year. Revolut's UK deposit base is forecast to swell by £40 billion over the next three to four years. That is not a rounding error. It is a structural shift in who holds the money.

## The marketing gap — it is not about money. It is about discipline.

The instinct of every incumbent when they see these numbers is to spend more. Buy more ads. Increase the budget. Outspend the challenger.

That instinct is wrong. The gap between neobanks and incumbents is not a spend problem. It is a narrative problem.

Fintech bank holding companies spend 8.45% of noninterest expense on marketing. Traditional banks spend 2.3% to 2.5%. That is a 3.5x gap. But the real question is not how much they spend. It is what they have to say.

Revolut spent zero on marketing for its first five to seven years. Zero. The company grew entirely on word-of-mouth. Today, 63% of new retail customers still arrive through referrals or organic channels. The product is the marketing. When a feature is good enough that users tell their friends, no advertising budget can match it. When it is not, no advertising budget can save it.

Now look at the incumbents. Santander spends an estimated £5.62 million per month on paid search — and generates fewer than 45,000 clicks. That is more than £100 per click. The average UK high street bank wastes £253,395 per month on paid search spend that delivers no return. Meanwhile, Monzo's help center ranks for 80,000 unique search queries — more than HSBC's entire UK retail banking site. Revolut ships programmatic landing pages in 30-plus languages, each capturing transactional intent at the moment a customer is deciding where to send money, open an account, or convert currency.

The neobanks treat their product features and help content as marketing assets. The incumbents treat marketing as a cost centre with a brochure.

And then there is the message itself. Incumbents advertise trust. They advertise heritage. They advertise stability. Nobody has ever switched banks because of trust. People switch because another product is genuinely better. Revolut does not advertise trust. It advertises interbank exchange rates, crypto trading, multi-currency accounts, and airport lounge access. Monzo does not advertise heritage. It advertises Salary Sorter, Tax Pots, and real-time spending notifications that actually change how people manage money. The challengers advertise features worth switching for. The incumbents advertise reasons to stay — and customers are not listening.

## The super app question — no, you do not need to be everything.

The instinct of every incumbent board when they see Revolut is: "We need to build a super app." Mobile plans. Insurance. Crypto. Travel. ESIMs. Everything in one place.

That is the wrong lesson.

Revolut did not start as a super app. It started with one killer feature: interbank foreign exchange rates for travelers. That was the product. A card that gave you the real exchange rate when you spent abroad. People talked about it. They told their friends. Sixty-three percent of growth came from referrals. Then Revolut added features one by one — each one a self-contained product that customers discovered organically, not because Revolut forced it on them, but because the next product was genuinely useful once you already had the account. The company's chief growth officer calls this "snackable products." You come for one thing. You stay for another. Eventually, you consolidate your entire financial life.

Monzo started with a coral card and instant spending notifications on your phone the moment you tapped it. That was the product. People talked about it at dinner. The budgeting tools — Salary Sorter, Pots, round-ups — came after the core experience earned trust.

Starling started with a great current account. Then it built Engine, a platform that sells its core banking technology to other banks. The challenger became an infrastructure provider. That is not a super app. That is a focused strategy built on one capability done brilliantly.

The pattern is consistent across every neobank that has succeeded: pick one thing. Make it undeniable. Let the product be the marketing. If people do not talk about your product, no amount of ad spend will fix it.

Incumbents do the opposite. They try to be everything to everyone from day one. They offer mortgages, current accounts, credit cards, savings accounts, insurance, investments, business banking — and none of it is remarkable. A customer with a mortgage, a current account, and a credit card at a big bank has no reason to stay loyal. None of those products are worth mentioning to a friend. None of them create the kind of experience that makes someone open the app and think, "I could not live without this."

The answer is not to build a super app. The answer is to pick one capability and make it the best in your market. If you are a mortgage bank, make the mortgage experience so seamless that people tell their friends about the application process. If you are a payments bank, make transfers so fast and so cheap that nobody considers alternatives. If you are a savings bank, make the rate so competitive that it becomes the default place to put money.

One thing. Undeniable. Worth talking about.

## What the CTO should do.

The CTO who wins in this environment understands three things.

First, the product is the marketing. If the engineering team builds something remarkable, customers will advertise it for free. Revolut proved this for five years with zero marketing spend. The CTO who does not understand this will keep approving brochure campaigns while Revolut poaches their customers one referral at a time.

Second, speed beats perfection. Revolut's chief growth officer talks about "velocity over certainty" — making decisions with forty percent of the information in forty seconds. Most bank CTOs need six months of vendor evaluation before approving a feature. By the time they act, the market has moved twice. The CTO who cannot ship fast enough to matter will watch the neobanks ship three features in the time it takes to form a steering committee.

Third, one thing done brilliantly beats ten things done adequately. The CTO who tries to build everything will build nothing well. The CTO who picks one capability — lending, payments, FX, savings — and makes it the best in market gives the bank something worth talking about. That is worth more than any marketing budget. Because the best marketing is a product so good that customers cannot help but tell someone about it.

## The challenger era is over.

Revolut, Monzo, and Starling are not challengers anymore. They are banks. They have licences, balance sheets, regulatory approval, and profitability. They are opening offices in new European markets, hiring ex-incumbent executives, and taking market share directly from the institutions that once dismissed them as experiments.

The question is no longer whether incumbents will be disrupted. The disruption already happened. The question is whether incumbents will give customers a reason to stay — or keep spending £5.62 million a month on search ads that no one clicks, while the product they are advertising becomes less relevant every quarter.

That reason starts with the product. And the product starts with the CTO.
