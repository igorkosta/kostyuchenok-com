# Revolut Keeps Getting Breached. The Fines Were Never the Problem.

On Friday, September 11, 2026, Revolut emailed a small number of its more than 80 million customers to tell them something that should not be possible at a bank: their passports, driver's licenses, selfies and financial records were no longer theirs alone. The notification listed what the company had disclosed. Dates of birth. Postal and email addresses. Phone numbers. Copies of identity documents. And, potentially, verification selfies, account statements and transaction histories.[1]

A day earlier, Revolut had received a request for customer information that appeared to come from a government agency. The domain was genuine. The message carried valid technical authentication. By the company's own description, the email passed the checks designed to confirm a message really comes from a government authority. Revolut staff treated it as a routine legal request and released the file.[1][2]

That is the detail that matters. The hackers did not break into Revolut's database. They did not defeat a firewall or steal a key. They asked, and a bank — a bank that now holds deposit-taking licences on two continents — handed over the exact package of information a regulated fintech is required to collect about its customers, to whoever produced the best-looking email.[2][3]

By the weekend, the recipients had stopped hiding. Reporting first circulated that the attackers had begun publishing customer files, including identity documents and verification selfies of high-profile clients: Alexander Shevchenko, the tennis player, and Felix Römer, chief executive of the crypto casino Gamdom. On Telegram the attackers announced a schedule — more data published every day until Revolut "pays for leaking their customers" — and accused the company of negligence and of handing customer data to parties outside its proper jurisdiction.[4][5]

The ransom, as reported across crypto and mainstream outlets, was 10,000 Bitcoin. At mid-September prices near $77,600 per coin, that is roughly $780 million, which is not a number a company negotiates. It is a number a company ignores or a number a company's reputation absorbs. Revolut has not said which.[5][6]

City AM reported on Tuesday that the company has contacted the roughly 680 people affected by what it called a "sophisticated" impersonation scam. The compromised records included identity documents, postal and email addresses, facial-verification images and phone numbers. Revolut has not confirmed a public figure itself, saying only that a "limited number" of customers were involved and that its systems and customer funds were unaffected.[7]

The message a high-net-worth customer reads in that notification is worse than "your data leaked." It is "your data was assembled into a dossier, placed inside a process designed to release it on request, and given away." The affected file, per affected-customer notices reviewed by the press, ran to IBANs, account statements, withdrawal records and full transaction histories, including all Bitcoin activity. Onchain investigator ZachXBT said the incident appeared targeted, possibly aimed at wealthy customers; security researchers described it as profiling of high-value individuals using their KYC documents and cryptocurrency transaction records tied to their real identities.[8][15]

It is tempting to call this a sophisticated attack. In the final delivery it was not — a well-written email on a real domain, sent to a process built to say yes. The sophistication was in choosing the target and in preparing the ground to hit it, and in recognizing that a certain kind of fintech will release what it holds if the request looks right. That recognition is earned. Revolut has spent the last five years proving it.

According to a person who identifies themselves as behind the operation, the public timeline understates the campaign. The group told this publication that it spent roughly six months positioning itself first — compromising the IT infrastructure of agents of the Italian government, planting remote access trojans on the agents' machines — before using that foothold to send the information requests from genuinely functioning government mail systems. Public reporting has not confirmed this account, Revolut has named neither the agency nor the country, and an anonymous source talking up its own operation is thin evidence on its own. It is worth stating because it fits the observable facts: the attackers' Telegram messages accused Revolut of handing customer data to parties outside the proper jurisdiction, and the notified customers spanned countries over which no Italian authority has any remit.[4][5] If the account is accurate, the six months is the most damning part — an information-request process stayed open to a compromised government mailbox for half a year, and no second verification ever triggered.[17]

**The pattern: incident, fine, patch, repeat.**

The public record is not a run of bad luck. It is a run of incidents that share a shape: incident, apology, regulator action, narrow fix, next incident.

In early 2022, criminals stole more than $20 million of Revolut's own money through a payment flaw caused by differences between its US and European systems. When certain transactions were declined, the company erroneously refunded them from its own funds, and organized groups exploited it for months, withdrawing the refunds at ATMs. Total take: about $23 million, netted to roughly $20 million after recovery. The company has never disclosed this publicly; it came to light only when the Financial Times reported it in 2023.[10]

In 2022, the teenage hacking gang Lapsus$ compromised Revolut. The BBC's later account of the group's trial noted the teens had "helped attack Revolut," among a list that included Microsoft and Uber. A breach of a bank by teenagers barely moved the needle at the time.[11]

In September 2022, a social-engineering attack gave a third party unauthorized access to Revolut's customer database, exposing the personal data of 50,150 customers worldwide — 20,687 of them in the European Economic Area. Names, addresses, emails, phone numbers, account details. Lithuania's State Data Protection Inspectorate opened an investigation, and a phishing campaign imitating Revolut followed the breach. The company's response then was a statement to the effect that customers' money was safe — "as it always has been."[12]

The regulatory ledger tells the same story as the security ledger. In March 2022, the Bank of Lithuania issued warnings and fines of €50,000 and €150,000 against Revolut Bank and its payments arm for failures in anti-money-laundering controls around onboarding risk factors and politically exposed persons. Later that year, another €70,000 fine and a warning over complaint handling and late financial statements. In January 2024, a €200,000 fine for prudential requirements — the large-exposure rule. And in April 2025, the large one: a €3.5 million penalty, the largest ever issued by Lithuania's central bank, for failures in monitoring business relationships and operations that resulted in Revolut "not always properly identifying suspicious monetary operations or transactions carried out by customers in practice."[9]

That last fine is the most damning, because it is the core function of a bank. Revolut, the institution that collects everybody's passports and selfies and Bitcoin histories, was found by its own supervisor to be not always properly identifying suspicious activity among its customers. The 2025 finding describes a control function that monitors the customers it is required to monitor. It says nothing about whether the same controls would catch the counterparty in an identity-theft dossier transaction — because they were not designed to.

Around the same period, the auditor BDO was unable to independently verify three-quarters of the €636 million in revenue Revolut reported in its delayed 2021 accounts, and the design of its IT systems was flagged as putting a material part of that revenue at risk of misstatement. The UK banking licence Revolut applied for in 2021 dragged through a restricted "mobilisation" phase, with a deposit cap of £50,000, questions about money moving out of accounts the National Crime Agency had flagged, and meddlesome interference at the political level. A full licence finally arrived in March 2026.[13][14]

Count the pattern. The 2022 payment flaw was closed in the spring of 2022 — after tens of millions of dollars had left. The 2022 database breach was followed by assurances and an apology. Each regulator finding was addressed as an isolated procedural deficiency, corrected with a submission, and settled. Not one of these incidents produced a visible structural change to how Revolut holds, protects, and agrees to release the crown jewels of its business: the KYC dossier.

**The root cause is a design decision, not a lapse.**

The 2026 incident was not a security failure in the narrow sense. Revolut's statement is technically true: its systems were not compromised and no customer funds were lost. But that framing misses the point, as security researchers were quick to argue. The attackers did not need to compromise the systems because the system was designed to hand data over.[3]

Two design decisions made this breach inevitable.

First, authentication was treated as authorization. An email that originates from a genuine government domain and passes technical checks proves the message came from that domain. It does not prove that a valid legal request is behind it, nor that the person operating the account is who the agency says. Revolut's information-request process appears to have had no second layer: no independent verification of the request's validity, no human escalation for high-value or cross-border disclosures, no cryptographic or out-of-band confirmation with the named agency. When every technical box ticks, the process approves. Attackers who can obtain access to a government mail system buy themselves a ticket to every dossier in the file — and, if the claimed six-month campaign against Italian government infrastructure is accurate, that is exactly what happened. Email authentication cannot catch that: the message genuinely came from the compromised system. Only a layer that verifies the legal validity of a request, independent of where the message originated, can.[2][3][17]

Second, the dossier exists to be handed over. Revolut is not unique in collecting identity documents, selfies, IBANs, account statements and years of transaction history. It is close to unique in having a process engineered to release the entire package to anyone whose envelope looks official, and in keeping the full archive at hand for release. Data minimization and retention limits are not compliance trivia; they are the difference between "attackers got some names" and "attackers got a complete identity-theft and extortion kit for wealthy customers, including their Bitcoin activity."[8]

The extortion math shows where the industry's trust failures now price themselves. A few million in fines, occasionally. Hundreds of millions — 10,000 BTC worth — demanded over a weekend for identity files. The attackers are not asking Revolut to pay for the breach. They are asking Revolut to pay to prevent a daily leak of material so sensitive that victims' real-world safety and financial lives are on the line. The moment a bank's KYC archive becomes salable extortion ammunition, the bank has built a liability, and the only question is who invoiced it first.[4][6]

**Why it never gets fixed: the fines are cheaper than the redesign.**

Revolut is a remarkable company by any measure except this one. It grew from a money-transfer app to an 80-million-customer, multi-licence financial institution in a decade, building products people genuinely like and expanding into markets where it has no business being late. That growth was engineered, and it was engineered around the customer. Security, compliance and data governance were engineered in the margins, one regulator reproach at a time.

The pattern persists because it is rational under the incentives. A fine is a line item; a redesign is a quarter of a year of every engineering team's time, an audit of every process that touches personal data, and a decade of accumulated architecture that nobody wants to open. As long as the cost of an incident is capped by a regulator's arithmetic, the fix is to donate to the regulator's statistics and move on. Revolut's executives learned exactly how much a breach costs: €50,000 here, €200,000 there, €3.5 million for the big one. That is the list price of not having built the problem out of the system in the first place.

What a root fix would look like is known and unremarkable. Collect less and retain it for shorter periods. Encrypt at rest with keys a process cannot pass along with a file. Make high-value disclosures impossible without a second, independent approval. Verify "government" requests out-of-band, with the named agency, before touching a single record — signature-elevation as a default, not an afterthought. Treat the KYC dossier as the crown jewels of the institution, with the same controls, tamper-evidence and audit trail as a trading book. Institutions that internalized the principle that data is a liability — that data the bank no longer holds cannot be extorted — do not have this problem at this scale.

**The stakes just went up, and so did the invoices.**

The timing is the story that Revolut cannot paper over. The company is pressing ahead with a launch in Israel, was granted conditional approval by the US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency to establish a national bank, and is reportedly weighing a listing at a valuation around $200 billion.[5][16] In March 2026 it became a fully licenced UK bank holding FSCS-protected deposits — real depositor money, regulated now in the conventional sense. A bank that leaks its KYC archive to an extortionist in exchange for one well-formed email does not merely have a security problem; it has a licencing problem, a deposit problem and a valuation problem, distributed across every market it wants to enter next.[16]

The other invoice is already arriving. Every passport and selfie now circulating is a standing financial instrument for as long as it stays valid. The attackers say more will come daily. Even if Revolut pays nothing — and it almost certainly should not pay a Bitcoin ransom to anonymous extortionists — the cost cannot be contained by blocking one email address, which is the response the company has described so far.

The fines were never the problem. They were the price of the problem, paid on credit, with the interest compounding into reputational capital and now into the personal lives of specific customers. Revolut has scale, products and licences. What it has never built is a culture in which the question "could we be tricked today?" is a design requirement rather than a regression test. Until the data that makes those tricks profitable stops being hoarded and starts being protected as the crown jewel it is, the next request from a real-looking domain is already in the queue — and this time the invoice is denominated in passports, not euros.
